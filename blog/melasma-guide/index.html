Melasma: Why It's Harder to Fade Than Regular Dark Spots

Melasma looks like other dark patches at a glance, but it behaves differently — driven deeper by hormones and reactivated by even brief sun exposure most people wouldn't think twice about.

K Kloré Skin • Updated August 5, 2026 • 6 min read

Quick answer: Melasma is a hormonally-driven pigmentation condition, usually appearing as symmetrical brown or grayish patches on the cheeks, forehead, or upper lip, often triggered by pregnancy, birth control, or hormonal shifts. Unlike regular sun spots or PIH, melasma is extremely sun-reactive — even minimal, brief UV exposure can undo months of fading progress, which is why sun protection matters more here than for almost any other pigmentation concern.

What Makes Melasma Different

Melasma is driven by hormonal changes affecting melanin-producing cells, typically triggered by pregnancy, hormonal birth control, or hormone therapy. Unlike PIH from a healed breakout, melasma isn't caused by inflammation — it's a distinct pigmentation response that tends to appear symmetrically, often on the cheeks, forehead, nose bridge, or upper lip, and it's notably more resistant to fading than other forms of hyperpigmentation.

Common Triggers

  • Pregnancy, sometimes called the "mask of pregnancy" when melasma appears during this time.
  • Hormonal birth control or hormone replacement therapy.
  • Sun exposure, which can trigger new patches or worsen existing ones significantly.
  • Heat exposure, which some research suggests may contribute alongside UV light.

Why Sun Discipline Matters More Here

Melasma is unusually sensitive to both UV and visible light, meaning even brief, incidental sun exposure — a walk to the car, sitting near a bright window — can reactivate or worsen it in a way that doesn't happen as readily with regular dark spots. A daily, generously applied, broad-spectrum sunscreen (ideally tinted, since iron oxide blocks visible light that standard sunscreen doesn't) is the single most important factor in whether melasma treatment actually holds.

Common Mistakes

  • Using standard, untinted sunscreen and still seeing melasma worsen from visible light exposure.
  • Expecting the same fading timeline as regular PIH, when melasma is typically slower and more stubborn.
  • Skipping sunscreen on cloudy or indoor days, when melasma can still be triggered by incidental exposure.
  • Not considering a dermatologist consultation for a condition that often responds better to prescription-strength options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will melasma go away on its own?

Sometimes, particularly pregnancy-related melasma, which can fade after hormone levels return to baseline — though it can also persist and require active management.

Is melasma the same as regular sun spots?

No, sun spots are typically caused directly by cumulative UV damage, while melasma is hormonally driven and tends to be more symmetrical and resistant to fading.

Do I need tinted sunscreen for melasma?

It's commonly recommended, since standard sunscreens block UV but not visible light, which is a known trigger for melasma specifically.

Should I see a dermatologist for melasma?

It's a reasonable step, since prescription options (like certain topical combinations) are often more effective for melasma than over-the-counter products alone.

Final Thoughts

Melasma responds to the same fading ingredients as other pigmentation, but with much less room for error on sun protection. Consistent, generous, ideally tinted sunscreen use is what determines whether the rest of the routine actually gets a chance to work.
