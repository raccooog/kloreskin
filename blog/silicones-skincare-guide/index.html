Silicones in Skincare: Do They Actually Clog Pores?

Dimethicone gets blamed for breakouts across skincare forums constantly, but the ingredient itself has a very different reputation among dermatologists and formulators.

K Kloré Skin • Updated August 10, 2026 • 5 min read

Quick answer: Silicones like dimethicone are generally considered non-comedogenic — they sit on skin's surface rather than penetrating into pores, and most dermatological sources don't classify them as a common cause of clogged pores or breakouts. They can occasionally trap other pore-clogging ingredients underneath in a poorly formulated product, but the silicone itself is rarely the actual culprit.

What Silicones Actually Do in a Formula

Silicones like dimethicone create a smooth, silky texture, help products glide on evenly, and form a light, breathable film that can reduce moisture loss and temporarily blur the look of texture or fine lines. They're commonly found in primers, moisturizers, and sunscreens specifically because of this smoothing, texture-improving effect.

Where the Clogged Pore Claim Comes From

The concern likely stems from silicones forming a film on skin, which intuitively sounds like it could trap things underneath. In practice, most silicones used in skincare are considered non-comedogenic and don't have strong pore-clogging evidence behind them individually. When breakouts do follow a silicone-containing product, other ingredients in the same formula, or a failure to cleanse thoroughly at the end of the day, are more often the actual cause.

Who Might Genuinely Want to Avoid Them

  • Very oily skin that finds the film-forming texture feels heavy or exacerbates a shiny appearance.
  • Anyone who has personally noticed a pattern connecting silicone-heavy products to their own breakouts, regardless of the general research.
  • People preferring simpler, minimal-ingredient formulas for personal reasons unrelated to comedogenicity specifically.

This is more about personal preference and individual skin response than a universal reason to avoid the ingredient category — similar in spirit to how our guide to reading ingredient lists encourages checking your own patterns rather than following blanket rules.

Common Mistakes

  • Blaming a silicone-containing product for a breakout without checking other ingredients in the same formula.
  • Avoiding silicones entirely based on forum anecdotes rather than dermatological consensus.
  • Not cleansing thoroughly at the end of the day, allowing residue (silicone or otherwise) to sit on skin overnight.
  • Assuming all "film-forming" ingredients behave identically, when formulation quality varies significantly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is dimethicone bad for skin?

Most dermatological sources consider it non-comedogenic and generally well tolerated, though individual sensitivity always varies.

Do silicones prevent other products from absorbing?

They can create a temporary barrier, which is part of why they're often used as a later step in a routine (like a primer or the final layer) rather than applied before actives that need to penetrate.

Why do some people swear silicones cause their breakouts?

Individual skin response varies, and other ingredients or insufficient cleansing are common alternative explanations worth ruling out before attributing it specifically to silicone content.

Are silicone-free products automatically better?

Not inherently — it comes down to personal preference and individual skin response rather than a general rule that silicone-free formulas outperform others.

Final Thoughts

Silicones have a rougher online reputation than their actual research supports. For most people, they're a functional, well-tolerated ingredient category — worth avoiding only if your own skin has shown a genuine, individual pattern of reacting to them.
