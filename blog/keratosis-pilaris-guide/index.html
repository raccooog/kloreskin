Keratosis Pilaris: What Those Bumps on Your Arms Actually Are

Small, rough, skin-colored bumps on the upper arms are extremely common, extremely misunderstood, and won't respond to a single thing marketed for acne.

K Kloré Skin • Updated August 4, 2026 • 5 min read

Quick answer: Keratosis pilaris (often called "chicken skin") happens when keratin builds up and plugs hair follicles, creating small, rough bumps, most commonly on the upper arms. It's a genetic, chronic condition, not an infection or acne, and it can't be permanently cured — but consistent use of chemical exfoliants (lactic or glycolic acid) and rich moisturizers can significantly reduce how visible and rough it feels.

What Keratosis Pilaris Actually Is

Keratosis pilaris happens when keratin, a protein naturally present in skin, builds up and forms a plug at the opening of a hair follicle. This creates the small, rough, often skin-colored or slightly red bumps typically found on the upper arms, though it can appear on the thighs or cheeks as well. It's extremely common, genetically linked, and considered a harmless, chronic skin variation rather than a disease or infection.

Why It's Not Acne, and Why Acne Treatment Won't Help

Unlike acne, keratosis pilaris isn't primarily driven by excess oil or bacteria — it's a keratin buildup issue. This is why benzoyl peroxide and other bacteria-targeting acne treatments generally don't help here, and why the more relevant approach borrows from exfoliation strategies instead, similar in principle to what's covered in our AHA vs BHA guide, just applied to a different underlying mechanism.

What Actually Helps

Approach Why It Helps
Lactic or glycolic acid lotion (AHA) Gently dissolves the keratin plug over consistent use
Urea-based moisturizers Softens rough texture and supports hydration
Gentle, non-abrasive cleansing Avoids further irritating already sensitive follicles
Consistent daily moisturizing Reduces the dryness that tends to make bumps more visible

Setting Realistic Expectations

What to Expect

  • ✔ Improvement is gradual, typically visible after several weeks of consistent treatment
  • ✔ It tends to fluctuate seasonally, often worse in dry winter months
  • ✔ It's manageable, not permanently curable — maintenance treatment is ongoing
  • ✔ It often improves somewhat with age on its own, independent of treatment

Common Mistakes

  • Using harsh physical scrubs, which can irritate the already sensitive follicles further.
  • Expecting acne treatments like benzoyl peroxide to help, when the underlying mechanism is different.
  • Stopping treatment once bumps improve, rather than continuing the maintenance routine that got them there.
  • Picking or squeezing at the bumps, which can cause irritation or scarring without resolving the underlying keratin buildup.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can keratosis pilaris be cured permanently?

No, it's a chronic condition that can be managed and significantly improved with consistent treatment, but it generally requires ongoing maintenance rather than a one-time fix.

Does keratosis pilaris get worse in winter?

Yes, dry air and reduced humidity commonly make it more noticeable, similar to how many dryness-related skin conditions fluctuate seasonally.

Is keratosis pilaris contagious?

No, it's a genetic, non-infectious skin variation, not caused by bacteria or anything transmissible.

Can diet affect keratosis pilaris?

There's limited strong evidence connecting diet directly to keratosis pilaris specifically, unlike the more studied connection between diet and acne.

Final Thoughts

Keratosis pilaris responds to patience and the right category of ingredient — chemical exfoliation and rich moisture — rather than anything marketed for acne. Consistent, gentle maintenance over weeks and months makes the real, visible difference here.
