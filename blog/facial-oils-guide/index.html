Facial Oils: Which Ones Actually Suit Your Skin

"Facial oil" covers dozens of genuinely different ingredients — some sit lightly on skin, others clog pores readily, and the difference isn't obvious from the marketing.

K Kloré Skin • Updated August 12, 2026 • 6 min read

Quick answer: Facial oils vary widely in how likely they are to clog pores. Lighter, more skin-compatible oils like squalane, jojoba, and rosehip generally suit most skin types including oily; heavier oils like coconut oil are widely considered more pore-clogging for facial use. Oils are emollients — they soften and seal — so they work best layered over a hydrating step rather than used alone as the sole moisturizer.

What Facial Oils Actually Do

Facial oils are emollients — they soften the skin's surface and help reduce moisture loss by forming a light seal. What they don't do is add water to skin, which is why an oil applied to dry, dehydrated skin without a hydrating step underneath often feels like it's not working. The same humectant-then-seal logic from our hyaluronic acid guide applies directly here.

The Comedogenic Rating Question

Comedogenic rating scales (typically 0–5) are widely cited online, but they're based on older testing methods that don't always predict real-world behavior on human facial skin. They're a rough starting reference rather than a definitive answer — individual response varies enough that patch testing your own skin, as covered in our patch testing guide, tells you more than a chart does.

Oils by Skin Type

Oil Generally Suits Notes
Squalane All skin types, including oily Lightweight, stable — see our squalane guide
Jojoba oil Most skin types, including combination Closely resembles skin's own sebum
Rosehip oil Normal to dry, texture concerns Lighter than many botanical oils, oxidizes relatively quickly
Coconut oil Body, rather than face Widely considered more pore-clogging for facial use
Marula, argan oil Dry skin Richer, more emollient textures

How to Use Them Correctly

Quick Start

  • ✔ Apply after water-based serums and before or mixed into moisturizer, following thin-to-thick layering
  • ✔ A few drops is generally enough — more doesn't seal better, it just feels greasier
  • ✔ Pair with a humectant underneath rather than using oil alone on dry skin
  • ✔ Patch test before regular facial use, particularly with richer botanical oils

Our serum layering guide covers where oils fit into a multi-step routine in more detail.

Common Mistakes

  • Using a facial oil alone on dehydrated skin, without a hydrating step underneath for it to seal in.
  • Applying oil before water-based serums, blocking their absorption.
  • Treating comedogenic rating charts as definitive rather than a rough starting reference.
  • Using coconut oil on facial skin based on its popularity for body and hair use.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can oily skin use facial oils?

Yes, lighter options like squalane and jojoba are generally well tolerated by oily skin, though heavier botanical oils may feel excessive.

Do facial oils replace moisturizer?

Not usually — oils seal moisture in but don't add water, so most people still benefit from a hydrating step and often a moisturizer as well.

Are comedogenic ratings reliable?

They're a rough starting reference based on older testing methods, useful as a general guide but not a definitive prediction of how an oil will behave on your specific skin.

When should I apply facial oil in my routine?

After water-based serums, either before moisturizer or mixed into it, following the general thin-to-thick layering principle.

Final Thoughts

"Facial oil" is a category, not a single ingredient — and the differences between options within it matter more than whether to use one at all. Matching the oil's weight to your skin type, and layering it over hydration rather than instead of it, resolves most of the confusion.
