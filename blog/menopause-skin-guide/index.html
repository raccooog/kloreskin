Skin Changes During Menopause: What Actually Shifts

Skin can change faster during perimenopause and menopause than at almost any other life stage — and the reason is a specific, well-understood hormonal shift, not just "aging."

K Kloré Skin • Updated August 4, 2026 • 6 min read

Quick answer: Declining estrogen during perimenopause and menopause reduces collagen production, oil production, and skin's ability to hold moisture, often noticeably within the first few years. Skin tends to become drier, thinner, and less elastic during this window. Richer moisturizers, ceramides, and consistent retinol use tend to matter more here than at any earlier stage.

The Estrogen Connection

Estrogen plays a significant role in maintaining collagen production, skin thickness, and the skin's ability to retain moisture. As estrogen declines during perimenopause and menopause, collagen production drops measurably, sometimes by a meaningful percentage within the first several years of this transition — faster than the more gradual collagen decline associated with aging alone.

What Actually Changes

Change Why It Happens
Increased dryness Reduced oil production and moisture retention
Thinner-feeling skin Declining collagen and elastin
Reduced elasticity Structural collagen loss
More visible fine lines Combined effect of reduced collagen and hydration

Adjusting a Routine for This Stage

What Tends to Matter More Now

  • ✔ Ceramide-rich moisturizers, to address the barrier and moisture changes directly
  • ✔ Consistent retinol use, since collagen support becomes more valuable during this decline
  • ✔ Layered hydration — hyaluronic acid sealed with a richer moisturizer than before
  • ✔ Daily sunscreen, since thinner skin is more vulnerable to further UV-driven collagen loss

This is a good point to revisit our ceramides guide and retinol guide even for people who found those ingredients unnecessary earlier — the equation genuinely changes at this stage.

Why Acne Sometimes Returns

Hormonal fluctuation during perimenopause can trigger a return of acne for some people who hadn't experienced regular breakouts in years, following a pattern similar to what's covered in our hormonal acne guide. This can feel disorienting alongside increasing dryness elsewhere, but both are connected to the same underlying hormonal shift.

Common Mistakes

  • Continuing a lightweight, gel-based routine that suited oilier skin years earlier.
  • Assuming increased dryness or sensitivity means a product allergy rather than a hormonal shift.
  • Avoiding retinol due to sensitivity concerns, when a slow, careful introduction often still works well.
  • Not adjusting sunscreen and hydration habits as skin's baseline needs change.

Frequently Asked Questions

How quickly does skin change during menopause?

Collagen loss can be most rapid in the first several years of the transition, though the exact timeline and severity vary by individual.

Can retinol still be started at this stage?

Yes, with the same gradual introduction recommended for any new user — starting slowly still applies, regardless of age.

Does hormone therapy affect skin?

Some people notice changes in skin hydration and texture with hormone therapy, though this is a medical decision best discussed with a doctor rather than approached through skincare alone.

Is it normal for acne to return during perimenopause?

Yes, hormonal fluctuation during this transition can trigger breakouts even without a prior history of adult acne.

Final Thoughts

Skin during menopause isn't just "getting older" — it's responding to a specific, identifiable hormonal shift that calls for a genuinely adjusted routine, not just more of the same products used more intensely. Matching the routine to what's actually changing tends to make the real difference here.
